## Era II: Middle Ages (about 600–1455)

### Proposed scenes

**II-1 · Between Byzantium and Bulgaria (7th–12th century).**
Slavs settled across the central Balkans in the 6th and 7th centuries. Over the next five centuries
the region of today's Kosovo was ruled in turn by the Byzantine Empire and the First Bulgarian
Empire (9th century to the early 11th), and by Byzantium again after Basil II's victory over
Bulgaria in 1018. The name "Albanians" (Albanoi, Arbanitai) first appears in Byzantine writers in
the 11th century (Michael Attaleiates, Anna Komnene), referring to the lands of today's Albania.
*Sources: Fine EMB; Curta; Malcolm ch. 3; Attaleiates, History; Anna Komnene, Alexiad.*
[verify: the dates of Bulgarian control of the Kosovo region, which sources give differently]

**II-2 · The heart of the Serbian medieval state (late 12th–14th century).**
The Serbian ruler Stefan Nemanja took parts of Kosovo in the 1180s–1190s, and by the early 13th
century his successors held all of it. For the next two centuries Kosovo was a central region of
the Serbian state: rich in mines and farmland, with royal residences around Prizren. The seat of
the Serbian Church moved to Peja in the 13th century; it became a patriarchate in 1346, when
Stefan Dušan was crowned emperor (at Skopje). Dušan was buried in the monastery of the Holy
Archangels near Prizren, which he founded. *Sources: Fine LMB; Ćirković; Malcolm ch. 3.*
[verify: date of Nemanja's conquest; date the church's seat moved to Peja; Dušan's burial]
→ see **D22** (names of rulers).

**II-3 · Churches and monasteries (14th century).**
Serbian kings built the churches that still stand: the Church of the Holy Virgin in Prizren
(1306–07, on an older Byzantine church), Graçanica (about 1321, King Milutin), the monastery at
Deçan (1327–1335, begun by Stefan Uroš III and completed under Dušan; its master builder was a
Franciscan friar, Vita of Kotor), and the churches of the Patriarchate at Peja (13th–14th century).
Their frescoes are among the most important of Byzantine-era art. In 2004 and 2006 UNESCO inscribed
the four as a World Heritage Site and in 2006 placed them on its list of sites in danger.
*Sources: Ćurčić; UNESCO 724; Malcolm ch. 3.* [verify: construction dates; Fra Vita's inscription]
→ see **D12**, **D13**.

**II-4 · Who lived here: charters, mines and merchants (14th–15th century).**
Royal charters list the villages given to monasteries and the people in them. The Deçan charters
(1330) and the charter of the Holy Archangels (about 1348) record Serbian villages and also
Albanian and Vlach herding communities. Novo Brdo (Novobërda) was one of the richest silver-mining
towns in the Balkans, worked by Saxon miners from Central Europe and traded by merchants from
Dubrovnik, with its own mint and Catholic churches. Trepça and Janjeva were mining and trading
centres too. *Sources: Malcolm ch. 3; Fine LMB; Ćirković; the Deçan charter (published edition,
[verify which]).* [verify: the number of Albanian communities named in each charter]
→ see **D11** (this is the most consequential framing choice in Era II).

**II-5 · The Battle of Kosovo (15 June 1389).**
On the plain of Kosovo, north-west of today's Prishtina, an Ottoman army under Sultan Murad I met
a coalition led by Prince Lazar of Serbia, with Vuk Branković and troops sent by King Tvrtko of
Bosnia under Vlatko Vuković. Both Lazar and Murad were killed: Murad by a Christian knight who
reached him during or after the battle. Neither side won a clear victory, but Serbia could not
recover; within a few years its rulers were Ottoman vassals. Murad's tomb (Tyrbja e Sulltan
Muratit, near Mazgit) still stands. *Sources: Emmert; Fine LMB; Malcolm ch. 4.*
→ see **D14** (date), **D15** (participants and the knight), **D16** (the later myth).

**II-6 · The Ottoman conquest (1448–1455).**
In October 1448 the Ottomans defeated a Hungarian army under John Hunyadi on the same plain
(Skanderbeg's Albanian forces were on their way but did not arrive in time). In 1455 Sultan
Mehmed II besieged and took Novo Brdo; a young man from the area, Konstantin Mihailović, was
taken captive and later wrote one of the few eyewitness accounts. Prizren and Peja fell the same
year. *Sources: Fine LMB; Malcolm ch. 5; Mihailović; Inalcik.*
[verify: the dates of the 1448 battle (17–20 October); Novo Brdo's surrender date in 1455;
Skanderbeg's route]

### Decisions for Era II

**D11 · Who lived in medieval Kosovo.**
The two national historiographies disagree sharply. Serbian historiography holds that medieval
Kosovo was overwhelmingly Serbian and that Albanians were a small group who arrived mainly later.
Albanian historiography holds that Albanians lived here continuously in large numbers, and that
many appear in records under Slavic Christian names (Pulaha's argument). The documents themselves
show Albanians present, especially in the west and in herding communities, alongside a Serbian
majority in the villages the charters cover; the 1455 Ottoman register (Defter 1455) shows mostly
Slavic names, with some Albanian ones, and how to read those names is exactly what is disputed.
Malcolm takes a middle position and is criticised from both sides.
*Recommendation:* the caption states only what the documents record ("Serbian royal charters
list Serbian villages, and also Albanian and Vlach communities"), names Novo Brdo's Saxons and
Ragusans, and gives **no proportions**. The source tab says plainly that historians disagree on
the size of the Albanian population and names both readings.

**D12 · How to name the monuments.**
Under D10 the towns are Peja, Deçan, Graçanica and Prizren in both languages. For the buildings:
*Recommendation:* Albanian text uses the established Albanian forms: "Manastiri i Deçanit",
"Patrikana e Pejës", "Manastiri i Graçanicës", "Kisha e Shën Premtes" (the church in Prizren).
English text uses descriptive names built from those place names: "the Deçan monastery",
"the Patriarchate of Peja", "Graçanica monastery", "the Church of the Holy Virgin in Prizren".
The text says plainly that they were built by Serbian kings and belong to the Serbian Orthodox
Church. The UNESCO listing appears in the source tab under its official title, as a citation,
not in brackets in the text.

**D13 · The monuments today.**
The monasteries have been under international protection since 1999, the Prizren church was
damaged in March 2004, and their status is part of today's politics.
*Recommendation:* II-3 tells only the medieval story and the UNESCO inscription. What happened to
the monuments after 1999 belongs to Era VIII and will be asked in that era's note.

**D14 · The date of the battle.**
The battle was fought on 15 June 1389 in the Julian calendar then in use. It is commemorated on
28 June (Vidovdan) because the Serbian Orthodox Church still uses the Julian calendar, which today
runs 13 days behind. Many websites write "28 June 1389", which is not correct for 1389.
*Recommendation:* "15 June 1389" on screen and on the timeline; the source tab explains the
28 June commemoration in one line.

**D15 · Who fought, and who killed the Sultan.**
- *Albanians in the battle:* some later sources (the Muzaka family chronicle of 1510, and some
  Ottoman chronicles) mention Albanian lords on the Christian side; there is no contemporary
  confirmation. [verify which Ottoman chronicles, via Malcolm ch. 4]
- *The knight:* Serbian tradition names him Miloš Obilić (earlier Kobilić); the name appears only
  in later sources. An Albanian tradition calls him Millosh Kopiliq and holds he was Albanian;
  there is no documentary evidence either way.
- *The outcome:* contemporary reports in the West even announced a Christian victory. Historians
  agree it was not a clear win for either side in the short term.
*Recommendation:* the caption names the coalition as the sources do (Serbian and Bosnian forces),
says "a Christian knight" killed Murad, and does not name him or claim an ethnicity for him. The
source tab mentions the later reports of Albanian participants and the competing traditions about
the knight. If you prefer, the caption can add: *"Later sources say Albanian lords also fought on
the Christian side."*

**D16 · The battle's later life.**
In the centuries after, the battle became the centre of Serbian epic poetry and church memory,
and in the 19th and 20th centuries it was used to support Serbian political claims to Kosovo
(including the speech at the site in June 1989).
*Recommendation:* one sentence: *"In later centuries the battle became the heart of Serbian epic
poetry, and later of political claims to Kosovo."* The 1989 speech belongs to Era VI.

## Era III: Ottoman era (1455–1912)

### Proposed scenes

**III-1 · Ottoman rule (15th–16th century).**
Kosovo was organised into Ottoman districts (sanjaks) centred on Vushtrri and Prizren; land was
granted to cavalrymen as timars in return for military service, and registered in detailed tax
registers. Sultan Mehmed II's mosque in Prishtina (Xhamia e Mbretit, the Fatih or Imperial Mosque)
dates from 1461. Conversion to Islam was gradual: slow in the 15th–16th centuries, faster in the
17th. *Sources: Inalcik; Malcolm ch. 5–6; Defter 1455.*
[verify: the mosque's inscription date (865 AH); sanjak names and dates]
→ see **D17** (how to describe conversion).

**III-2 · Towns, bazaars and a restored patriarchate (16th–17th century).**
Prizren, Prishtina, Peja, Gjakova and Vushtrri grew as Ottoman towns with bazaars, mosques, baths
and bridges: in Prizren the Sinan Pasha Mosque (1615) and the hamam of Gazi Mehmed Pasha. In 1557
the Serbian Patriarchate of Peja was restored, with the support of the Grand Vizier Mehmed Pasha
Sokollu (Sokolović), a convert of Bosnian Serb origin. The traveller Evliya Çelebi described Prizren
and Vushtrri in 1660 and recorded words of Albanian.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 6; Evliya; Ćurčić; Elsie.* [verify: mosque and hamam dates; the year of
Evliya's journey through Kosovo]

**III-3 · 1689–1690: an Austrian army, an archbishop and an exodus.**
In 1689, during the Great Turkish War, a Habsburg army under General Piccolomini reached Kosovo and
Prizren. Many local Christians, Catholic Albanians and Orthodox Serbs, joined or supported it.
The Catholic Archbishop Pjetër Bogdani, author of *Cuneus Prophetarum* (1685), one of the first
major works of Albanian literature, backed the campaign and died in Prishtina that winter.
When the Ottomans struck back, Serbian Patriarch Arsenije III led a large number of people north
into Habsburg Hungary in 1690. *Sources: Malcolm ch. 8; Elsie (entries: Bogdani, Arsenije);
Vickers.* [verify: Piccolomini's death in Prizren, November 1689; Bogdani's death date]
→ see **D18** (most consequential framing choice in Era III).

**III-4 · 1877–78: refugees from the north.**
In the Serbian–Ottoman war of 1877–78 Serbian forces took the districts of Niš, Prokuplje,
Kurshumlia and Leskovac, and the Albanian population of those districts was expelled or fled.
Many of these refugees (muhaxhirë) settled in Kosovo. *Sources: Malcolm ch. 11; Vickers; Elsie;
Jagodić, "The Emigration of Muslims from the New Serbian Regions 1877/1878", Balkanologie 2 (1998).*
[verify] → see **D19** (numbers).

**III-5 · The League of Prizren (1878–1881).**
In March 1878 the Treaty of San Stefano, and that summer the Congress of Berlin, assigned lands
with Albanian populations to Serbia and Montenegro. On 10 June 1878, delegates from Albanian lands
met in Prizren and founded the League of Prizren to defend them. Its leaders included Abdyl
Frashëri, Ymer Prizreni and Sulejman Vokshi. It resisted the handover of Plav and Gusinje to
Montenegro, and under Frashëri's influence came to demand an autonomous Albanian province within
the Empire. In 1881 the Ottoman army suppressed it.
*Sources: Skendi; Gawrych; Frashëri; Malcolm ch. 12; Elsie.* [verify: the founding date; the
building where the delegates met; the date of the Ottoman capture of Prizren in April 1881]
→ see **D20**.

**III-6 · An unruly province (1881–1908).**
The Vilayet of Kosovo (1877–1912) was governed from Prishtina and, from 1888, from Skopje. Central
control was weak and the province was insecure for its inhabitants. In 1899 Haxhi Zeka called the
League of Peja, which again sought Albanian self-government. Schools in Albanian were forbidden by
the Ottoman authorities, and the language was taught in secret. In 1908 the Young Turk revolution
promised reform; many Albanians first supported it.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 12; Gawrych; Skendi; Elsie.* [verify: Vilayet dates; capital moved 1888]
→ see **D21**. *(If v1 must stay near 40 scenes, this scene can be folded into III-5 and III-7.)*

**III-7 · The uprisings of 1910–1912.**
In 1910 an Albanian revolt against the Young Turk government broke out in Kosovo; Idriz Seferi
held the Kaçanik pass for a time. Revolts followed in 1911. In 1912 a general uprising led by Hasan
Prishtina, Isa Boletini, Bajram Curri and others took Prishtina and, in August, Skopje; the
government accepted most of their demands. Two months later the Balkan Wars began (Era IV, IV-1).
*Sources: Gawrych; Skendi; Malcolm ch. 12; Elsie.* [verify: the date the insurgents entered
Skopje, about 12 August 1912; the demands accepted]

### Decisions for Era III

**D17 · How to describe conversion to Islam.**
Historians describe a gradual process driven largely by incentives: Muslims paid lower taxes (no
cizye, the poll tax on non-Muslims) and could enter the Ottoman administration and army. Episodes
of pressure, especially after the wars of the late 17th century, are recorded too, and Albanian
popular memory stresses coercion. Some families kept Christian faith in secret while living as
Muslims (the *laramanë*, "the motley ones").
*Recommendation:* *"Over two centuries, most Albanians, and some Serbs, became Muslim, mainly for
the lower taxes and the careers Islam opened; at times under pressure."* The *laramanë* are
held for the Culture channel.

**D18 · 1690: the "Great Migration".**
Serbian tradition and historiography speak of a mass exodus of Serbs from Kosovo under Patriarch
Arsenije III; the figure of 30,000–40,000 families (or about 37,000) goes back to later
church tradition, and some writers conclude that Kosovo was emptied of Serbs and resettled by
Albanians. Malcolm argues the numbers are exaggerated, that most migrants came from further north,
and that Albanians also took part in the revolt and the flight; Serbian historians reject his
reading. Contemporary documents do not give a reliable count.
*Recommendation:* consistent with D8: no figure. State what is agreed (the Austrian advance,
local Christians of both peoples supporting it, the Ottoman reprisals, Arsenije leading many people
north in 1690); the source tab states the two readings. Also: **do not use Paja Jovanović's
*Migration of the Serbs* (1896)** as the image: it is a national-romantic painting made two
centuries later. Use the title page of Bogdani's *Cuneus Prophetarum* (1685) or a period map of
the campaign.

**D19 · 1877–78: how many Albanians were expelled.**
Estimates of Albanians expelled from the Niš–Toplica region differ widely between Serbian and
Albanian sources, and between historians; I have not yet checked both ends to the page.
*Recommendation:* state the expulsion as fact (it is not in dispute) and give **no figure**,
as under D8. If you want a range, I will collect both ends with page references first.

**D20 · The League of Prizren: what it was.**
Albanian historiography presents the League as the founding moment of the Albanian national
movement. Most historians outside Albania (Skendi, Gawrych, Malcolm) agree it was a turning point,
and add that it began as an assembly of mostly Muslim notables, encouraged at first by the Ottoman
government to resist the loss of territory, and that its demand for Albanian autonomy grew within
it, through Abdyl Frashëri and others, which is why Istanbul crushed it.
*Recommendation:* tell that development honestly: *"It began as a defence of Albanian lands,
at first with the Sultan's blessing. It ended by demanding an autonomous Albania, and the Sultan's
army crushed it."* This keeps the national significance and stays accurate.

**D21 · 1878–1912: violence against Kosovo Serbs.**
In the decades after 1878, with weak government and the arrival of the refugees of III-4, Serbian
villagers in Kosovo suffered attacks and land seizures; Serbian consuls and some foreign observers
recorded them, and many Serbs left for Serbia. Serbian sources give emigration figures in the tens
of thousands; they are contested. Separately, Albanians of the vilayet suffered Ottoman punitive
expeditions (1910–12) and the ban on Albanian schooling.
*Recommendation:* one sentence in III-6, attributed and without a number: *"In these lawless
decades, Serbian consuls reported attacks on Serbian villagers, and many Serbs left for Serbia."*
This is a new period, not the 1941–45 question settled by D9. **Your call.**

**D22 · Names of medieval and Ottoman people.**
*Recommendation:* Albanian text uses the forms usual in Albanian historiography (Stefan Nemanja,
Stefan Dushani, Knez Lazari, Sulltan Murati I, Mehmeti II); English text uses the standard English
forms (Stefan Nemanja, Stephen Dušan, Prince Lazar, Murad I, Mehmed II). Personal names are not
place names, so D10 does not decide this.

**D23 · Era II length, and v1's total.**
Eras I and IV have 13 scenes; II and III as proposed add 13, leaving about 14 for Eras V–IX if v1
stays near 40. *Recommendation:* accept 6 + 7 now; if V–IX need more room, fold III-6 into its
neighbours and II-1 into II-2.
