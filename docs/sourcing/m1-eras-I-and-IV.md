## Era I: Antiquity

### Proposed scenes

**I-1 · The Dardani (4th century BC).**
A people known as the Dardani lived in the central Balkans, in a region ancient writers called
Dardania, which included most of today's Kosovo. They appear in Greek and Roman writers from the
4th century BC onward. *Sources: Papazoglu; Wilkes; Strabo, Geography 7.5.* → see **D1**.

**I-2 · A kingdom between Macedon and Rome (3rd–2nd century BC).**
Dardanian rulers named in ancient sources, Longarus, his son Bato, and Monunius, fought
repeated wars with the kingdom of Macedon. *Sources: Livy 31.28 (Bato, c. 200 BC); Polybius;
Papazoglu; Wilkes.* [verify exact passages] → see **D2** (Bardylis).

**I-3 · Rome (1st century BC to 1st century AD).**
Roman armies subdued Dardania during the 1st century BC, and it became part of the province of
Moesia, later Moesia Superior. Mining was central to the Roman economy here.
*Sources: Mócsy; Wilkes.* [verify dates: campaign of C. Scribonius Curio in the 70s BC; Moesia Superior created AD 86]

**I-4 · Ulpiana (2nd century AD).**
Near today's Prishtina (at Graçanica/Lipjan), the settlement of Ulpiana was raised to a Roman
municipium under Emperor Trajan (ruled AD 98–117), whose family name, Ulpius, it carries.
*Sources: Mócsy; Elsie; site publications of the Archaeological Institute of Kosovo.* [verify]

**I-5 · Dardania as a province; Justinian rebuilds (late 3rd to 6th century).**
Under Diocletian, Dardania became a province of its own. After an earthquake in 518, Emperor
Justinian rebuilt Ulpiana and renamed it Iustiniana Secunda. From the late 6th century, Avar
and Slav incursions ended Roman city life in the region.
*Sources: Procopius, Buildings 4.1; Marcellinus Comes (earthquake of 518); Mócsy; Malcolm ch. 2.* [verify]
A mosaic inscription naming Justinian and Theodora was reported from the Ulpiana excavations in
2023–2025; press reports disagree on the year of discovery, so I would cite the excavation
team's own publication or leave it out. → see **D3** (emperors "of Dardanian origin").

### Decisions for Era I

**D1 · Who were the Dardani?**
Scholars disagree. Names found in western Dardania (roughly today's Kosovo) are mainly
Illyrian; in the east there are Thracian names. Some treat the Dardani as Illyrian, some as a
mixed Illyrian-Thracian people, and ancient authors sometimes list them separately from the
Illyrians (Papazoglu; Wilkes; Katičić's study of names).
*Recommendation:* say "a people of the central Balkans, usually counted among or alongside the
Illyrians", and state plainly in one caption that scholars debate their exact kinship.

**D2 · Bardylis.**
Bardylis (4th century BC) is often called a Dardanian king, but that identification is a modern
hypothesis; ancient sources call him an Illyrian king, and scholars disagree on his realm.
*Recommendation:* leave Bardylis out of v1, or mention him only as "sometimes identified as Dardanian".

**D3 · Illyrian–Albanian continuity, and "Dardanian" emperors.**
That Albanian descends from an ancient Balkan language of this region is widely accepted; that
this language was specifically Illyrian is the mainstream view in Albanian scholarship and
considered plausible by many linguists elsewhere, but it cannot be proven because Illyrian is
barely recorded, and other origins have been proposed. Separately, Constantine (born at Naissus,
today's Niš) and Justinian (born at Tauresium) were born in or near Dardania; calling them
"Dardanian" or "Albanian" goes beyond the evidence.
*Recommendation:* the site does not assert continuity as fact and does not deny it. One calm
caption, e.g.: "Many scholars link the Albanian language to the ancient languages of this
region; the evidence is too thin for certainty." Emperors are described by birthplace only.
**This is the single most consequential framing choice in Era I. Your call.**

**D4 · Where the story starts.**
The Goddess on the Throne (the symbol of Prishtina) is Neolithic, thousands of years before the
Dardani, and outside Era I's range as the brief defines it (the opening counter stops at 1000 BC).
*Recommendation:* keep v1 to the brief and start with the Dardani; hold the Goddess for the
CH3 Culture channel. Alternative: use her as the title image.

## Era IV: 1912–1945

### Proposed scenes

**IV-1 · 1912: the First Balkan War.**
In October 1912 the Balkan League went to war with the Ottoman Empire. Serbian forces took
Prishtina within weeks, and Montenegrin forces took Peja and Gjakova. Contemporary observers
recorded large-scale killing of Albanian civilians and the burning of villages.
*Sources: Carnegie 1914 (ch. 4); Malcolm ch. 13; Leon Trotsky's war reports (1912–13); Edith
Durham, The Struggle for Scutari (1914); Leo Freundlich, Albania's Golgotha (1913).* → see **D6**.
[verify the exact date Prishtina was taken; sources give 22 October 1912]

**IV-2 · 1913: borders drawn in London.**
Albania declared independence on 28 November 1912. The Conference of Ambassadors in London
(1912–13) recognised an Albanian state but left Kosovo to Serbia and Montenegro.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 13; Elsie; Vickers.*

**IV-3 · 1915–1918: the First World War.**
In 1915 the Serbian army retreated through Kosovo towards the Adriatic. Austria-Hungary and
Bulgaria occupied the region until 1918; in the Austro-Hungarian zone Albanian-language schools
were allowed. In 1918 Kosovo became part of the new Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 13–14; Elsie.* [verify]

**IV-4 · The Kaçak movement (1918–1927).**
Armed Albanian resistance to the new state, known as the Kaçak movement, was supported by the
Kosovo Committee founded in Shkodra in 1918. Its best-known leaders in Drenica were Azem Galica
(Azem Bejta, died 1924) and his wife Shote Galica (died 1927).
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 14; Elsie (entries: Kaçak movement, Galica); Vickers.* → see **D7**.

**IV-5 · Colonisation and emigration (1920s–1930s).**
The Yugoslav state settled Serb and Montenegrin colonists on land in Kosovo, including
confiscated land, and did not permit schooling in Albanian. Many Albanians emigrated, mainly to
Turkey. In 1938 Yugoslavia and Turkey signed a convention for the resettlement of 40,000
families; the Second World War prevented it from being carried out.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 14; Vickers; Elsie.* → see **D8** (numbers).
[verify: the 1937 Čubrilović memorandum, if mentioned, must be cited as a memorandum to a
Belgrade discussion club, not as state policy]

**IV-6 · 1941: occupation and partition.**
After the Axis invasion of Yugoslavia in April 1941, most of Kosovo was joined to
Italian-occupied Albania; the north, with the Trepça mines, came under German-occupied Serbia,
and an eastern strip under Bulgaria. Albanian-language schools opened. Serb and Montenegrin
settlers were attacked and driven out.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 15; Fischer; Ramet.* → see **D8**, **D9**.

**IV-7 · 1943–1944: German occupation.**
After Italy's capitulation in September 1943, Germany took control. In 1944 the SS formed the
Skanderbeg division from local recruits. In May 1944 its men arrested 281 Jews in Prishtina, who
were deported to Bergen-Belsen; many did not survive. [verify the count and the survival figure]
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 15; Fischer; EHRI (gives "about 280").* → see **D9**.

**IV-8 · 1944–1945: one war ends.**
At Bujan (31 December 1943 – 2 January 1944), Kosovo's communist-led resistance passed a
resolution that the people of Kosovo should be able to decide their own future, including union
with Albania; the Yugoslav party leadership rejected it. Partisan forces took Kosovo in late
1944. An uprising in Drenica led by Shaban Polluzha was suppressed in early 1945, military rule
was imposed, and in 1945 Kosovo was made an autonomous region within Serbia in the new Yugoslavia.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 15–16; Elsie (entries: Bujan Conference, Polluzha); Judah.* [verify dates] → see **D8**.

### Decisions for Era IV

**D6 · 1912–13: how to state the killings.**
That mass killings of Albanian civilians took place is documented by neutral contemporaries
(Carnegie 1914; Trotsky; Durham). The **number** is not established: a figure of about 20,000–
25,000 Albanians killed in the first months circulates widely and goes back to press reports and
Freundlich (1913), not to a count. Much higher totals for the whole of the Balkan Wars appear
online without solid sourcing.
*Recommendation:* state the killings and the burning of villages as fact, attribute them
("the Carnegie Commission reported…"), and give no total; if you want a number, give the
contemporary estimate **as an estimate, with its source named**.

**D7 · The Kaçaks: wording.**
Yugoslav authorities of the time called them outlaws; Albanian tradition honours them as
freedom fighters; historians describe an armed resistance movement with both political and
local roots.
*Recommendation:* "armed resistance", with Azem and Shote Galica named, no adjectives either way.

**D8 · Disputed numbers (colonisation, emigration, 1941, 1944–45).**
- Colonists settled between the wars: commonly about 60,000–70,000 people. [verify in Malcolm]
- Albanians and other Muslims who left for Turkey 1918–41: estimates range from under 100,000
  to about 250,000 depending on the source. [verify both ends and name each source]
- Serbs and Montenegrins expelled or fled after 1941: estimates range from about 20,000 to
  100,000; killed: from a few thousand to about 10,000. [verify both ends and name each source]
- Albanians killed in 1944–45: Albanian sources give tens of thousands; documented figures are
  far lower; no agreed number exists. [verify and name each source]

The ranges above are from my reading of the literature and are listed only to show how far
apart the sources are. None of them is checked to the page yet, and none will appear on the
site unless you ask for it and it is verified.
*Recommendation:* v1 states what happened and gives **no figure** where the range is this wide,
except where a caption can honestly say "estimates range from X to Y" with both ends sourced.
The source tab lists the competing estimates.

**D9 · The Second World War: including what is uncomfortable.**
For many Kosovo Albanians 1941 brought Albanian schools and administration after two decades of
repression, under an occupation; it also brought violence against Serb civilians, and in 1944
the deportation of Prishtina's Jews by a locally recruited SS unit. Albanians in Kosovo and
Albania also sheltered Jews. A trustworthy site has to hold all of these.
*Recommendation:* include all four facts, each in one plain sentence, as in IV-6 and IV-7 above.
**This is the most consequential framing choice in Era IV. Your call.**

### Place names

**D10 · Naming convention.** *Recommendation:* Albanian text uses Albanian names (Kosova,
Prishtina, Peja, Gjakova). English text uses "Kosovo" (the standard English name) and the
Albanian forms of town names (Prishtina, Peja, Gjakova), which is the practice of Kosovo's
institutions in English. On first mention of a town in the English text, the Serbian form
follows in brackets only where an international reader would otherwise be lost (e.g. "Peja (Peć)").
