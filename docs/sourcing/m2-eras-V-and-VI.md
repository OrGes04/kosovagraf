## Era V: In socialist Yugoslavia (1945–1989)

Treatment: **CRT** (black-and-white television), as agreed in the brief.

### Proposed scenes (6)

**V-1 · The Ranković years (1945–1966).**
After 1945 Kosovo was an autonomous unit within Serbia, but real power lay with the security
police under Aleksandar Ranković, Yugoslavia's interior minister. Albanians lived under close
surveillance. In the winter of 1955–56 a police campaign to seize weapons brought beatings and
arrests in Albanian villages. Under an arrangement with Turkey from 1953, many Albanians were
pressed to declare themselves Turks and emigrate.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 16; Vickers; Ramet; HPSH IV.* [verify: the 1955–56 campaign; the 1953
arrangement] → D30, D31.

**V-2 · 1966–1968: Ranković falls; "Kosova Republikë".**
In July 1966 Tito removed Ranković at the Brioni plenum, and the abuses of the security police in
Kosovo were admitted. On 27 November 1968 Albanian students and young people demonstrated in
Prishtina and other towns, calling for a university and for Kosovo to become a republic. The
demands for a republic were refused, but reforms followed: "Metohija" was dropped from the
province's name, and the Albanian flag could be flown. Adem Demaçi, imprisoned in 1958, 1964 and
1975, spent 28 years in Yugoslav prisons in all; in 1991 he received the European Parliament's
Sakharov Prize. *Sources: Malcolm ch. 16; Elsie (entries: Demaçi; 1968 demonstrations); HPSH IV.*
[verify: plenum date 1 July 1966; the 27 November 1968 demonstrations; the dates of Demaçi's
sentences] → D38.

**V-3 · 1970: the University of Prishtina.**
In February 1970 the University of Prishtina opened, teaching in Albanian and Serbo-Croatian. In
the years that followed, Albanian-language schools, the newspaper *Rilindja*, Radio Television
Prishtina and the Albanological Institute grew. In 1972 Kosovo's Albanians adopted the unified
literary language agreed at the Orthography Congress in Tirana.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 16; Elsie; Kostovicova.* [verify: opening date (15 February 1970); the 1972
congress and Kosovo's adoption]

**V-4 · 1974: the constitution.**
The Yugoslav constitution of 1974 made Kosovo a Socialist Autonomous Province with almost the
powers of a republic: its own constitution, assembly, government, courts and a seat in the federal
presidency. It was still part of Serbia, and was not a republic.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 16; Ramet; IICK; the Constitution of the SAP of Kosovo, Official Gazette
no. 4, 27 February 1974.* [verify dates]

**V-5 · 1981: the demonstrations.**
In March 1981 a student protest about conditions in the university canteen grew within weeks into
demonstrations across Kosovo, with the call "Kosova Republikë". The federal authorities declared a
state of emergency and sent in the army and police. Among those killed on 2 April 1981 in Prishtina
were the young demonstrators Naser Hajrizi and Asllan Pireva.
*Sources: Mertus; Malcolm ch. 16; Amnesty (1985); Elsie.* [verify: start date 11 March 1981; the
names and date on the Prishtina memorial plaque] → D32.

**V-6 · The 1980s: prisons and "differentiation".**
After 1981 thousands of Albanians were arrested, many sentenced to long terms for "verbal crime"
or nationalism, and teachers, students and officials were purged in a campaign of
"differentiation". In Belgrade, Serbian nationalists, among them the authors of the 1986 Memorandum
of the Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts, claimed that Serbs were being driven out of Kosovo;
Slobodan Milošević rose to power on these claims from 1987.
*Sources: Amnesty (1985 and later); Malcolm ch. 17; Mertus; Judah; Ramet.* [verify: numbers of
political prisoners in Amnesty's reports] → D33.

### Decisions for Era V

**D30 · Emigration to Turkey (1950s–1960s): how many?**
Estimates differ widely: Turkish immigration records count about 150,000 immigrants from all of
Yugoslavia in 1953–60 (not only Albanians); one scholarly estimate gives about 175,000 Albanians in
1952–66; Albanian sources go up to about 400,000. [verify each to the page]
*Recommendation:* no figure (D8). The caption says "many Albanians"; the source tab lists the
estimates with their sources.

**D31 · The 1955–56 weapons campaign.**
Well documented as brutal, but there is no reliable count of victims.
*Recommendation:* state it (beatings, arrests), no number.

**D32 · 1981: the death toll.**
Official Yugoslav figures gave between 9 and 12 dead (sources differ); Amnesty International noted
in 1985 that unofficial sources gave "far higher" figures. No independent count exists.
*Recommendation:* the caption names Naser Hajrizi and Asllan Pireva and says: "Official figures
admitted about ten dead; unofficial sources speak of many more." No precise number on screen.

**D33 · The Serbian campaign of the 1980s.**
The claim that Kosovo Serbs were being driven out was the central argument of Serbian nationalism
in the 1980s, and Milošević's rise cannot be explained without it.
*Recommendation:* one clause, presented as the claims Belgrade made (as in V-6), with no
assessment of them on screen. This stays within D9/D21: no scene of violence against Serbs.

**D38 · The province's names.**
Officially "Autonomous Region of Kosovo and Metohija" (1945), "Autonomous Province of Kosovo and
Metohija" (1963), "Socialist Autonomous Province of Kosovo" (1968/1974).
*Recommendation:* use "Kosova/Kosovo" throughout (D10). Mention "Metohija" only once, in V-2, as
the name that was dropped in 1968.

## Era VI: Autonomy revoked and the parallel system (1989–1998)

Treatment: **VHS**, with a camcorder date stamp (see D40).

### Proposed scenes (6)

**VI-1 · 1989: autonomy revoked.**
In February 1989 the miners of Trepça went on hunger strike underground, demanding that Kosovo's
autonomy be kept. In March, with tanks around the building, Kosovo's assembly approved the
amendments to Serbia's constitution that removed most of its autonomy; Serbia's assembly adopted
them on 28 March. Protests across Kosovo were met with force, and people were killed. On 28 June
1989, at the 600th anniversary of the battle of Kosovo, Milošević told a vast crowd at Gazimestan
that armed battles could not be ruled out.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 17; Judah; Mertus; Amnesty; HRW.* [verify: hunger strike 20–28 February
1989; assembly vote 23 March 1989; the wording of the Gazimestan speech] → D34, D16 (from Era II).

**VI-2 · 1990: the Kosovo Assembly declares; Serbia takes over.**
On 2 July 1990, locked out of their building, Albanian members of Kosovo's assembly declared on its
steps that Kosovo was an equal unit of Yugoslavia. Serbia dissolved the assembly days later. On 7
September 1990, meeting in secret in Kaçanik, the deputies adopted a constitution for a Republic of
Kosova. Serbia took over Kosovo's institutions: Albanians were dismissed en masse from public
jobs, hospitals and factories; the newspaper *Rilindja* and the Albanian programmes of Radio
Television Prishtina were shut down; Albanian schools were forced onto a Serbian curriculum.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 17; Clark; HRW (1992); Judah.* [verify all dates] → D35.

**VI-3 · 1990–1992: forgiving the blood.**
A movement of students and volunteers led by the folklorist Anton Çetta travelled from village to
village to end blood feuds. At gatherings attended by tens of thousands, such as at Verrat e Llukës
on 1 May 1990, families publicly forgave. Hundreds of feuds were reconciled.
*Sources: Clark; Elsie (entry: Çetta); HPSH IV.* [verify: the date and place of the largest
gathering; the number of feuds reconciled, which Albanian sources give as about 1,000; to be
checked in Clark before any number is shown]

**VI-4 · 1991–1992: the referendum and the Republic of Kosova.**
On 22 September 1991 the underground assembly declared Kosovo independent, and between 26 and 30
September a referendum was held in private homes and secret locations: 87 percent of registered
voters took part, and nearly all voted for independence. Only Albania recognised the Republic of
Kosova. On 24 May 1992, in elections Serbia declared illegal, Ibrahim Rugova of the Democratic
League of Kosovo (LDK) was elected president.
*Sources: Malcolm ch. 17; Clark; Judah; Elsie.* [verify: the official results (sources give
99.87% to 99.98% for "yes"; the caption says "nearly all"); Albania's recognition, 21–22 October
1991]

**VI-5 · The parallel state.**
Albanians built their own state in the shadows: schools and the university in private houses,
garages and mosques; clinics run by the Mother Teresa Society; a 3 percent voluntary contribution
from people in Kosovo and the diaspora in Germany, Switzerland and elsewhere paid teachers and
doctors. Rugova's strategy was non-violent resistance: to deny Serbia's rule without giving it a
pretext for war.
*Sources: Clark; Kostovicova; Judah; IICK.* [verify: the 3% contribution and how it was collected]
→ The diaspora part fits the site's audience; it can link to the later Daily life channel.

**VI-6 · 1995–1997: patience runs out.**
The Dayton peace agreement of November 1995 ended the war in Bosnia but left Kosovo out. A 1996
agreement to return Albanian students to their schools was never implemented. On 1 October 1997
tens of thousands of students marched peacefully in Prishtina and were dispersed by police. On 28
November 1997, at a funeral in Llaushë in Drenica, uniformed members of the Kosovo Liberation Army
(UÇK) appeared in public for the first time.
*Sources: Judah; Malcolm (1998 ed., epilogue); Clark; HRW.* [verify: the 1 September 1996
agreement; the 1 October 1997 march; the Llaushë funeral (of the teacher Halit Geci)] → D37.

### Decisions for Era VI

**D34 · March 1989: the death toll.**
Official figures gave 24 dead (22 demonstrators and 2 policemen) [verify, Amnesty]; Albanian
sources give higher numbers.
*Recommendation:* as D32: give the official figure, attributed, and say unofficial sources speak
of more; or no number at all. Your call.

**D35 · How many Albanians were dismissed?**
No single count: sector figures exist (e.g. university staff, teachers), Albanian leaders and some
reports give totals from tens of thousands to over 100,000, and Freedom House wrote of "hundreds of
thousands" losing their jobs.
*Recommendation:* no figure; "dismissed en masse" in the caption, the sector figures in the
source tab once verified.

**D36 · The 1990 "poisoning" of schoolchildren.**
In March 1990 thousands of Albanian schoolchildren fell ill. Albanians widely believe they were
poisoned; Serbian authorities called it mass hysteria; investigations at the time did not settle
it. [verify: what the investigations found]
*Recommendation:* leave it out of v1, because no finding can be stated as fact. Alternative: one
sentence saying it happened and that its cause was never established. Your call.

*Owner decision (2026-11-12): keep it; the owner holds that the pupils were poisoned.* What the
sources found so far say:
- Thousands of pupils fell ill (figures around 7,000–8,000 [verify]) with breathing difficulty,
  dizziness and fainting.
- A federal commission under the Slovenian doctor Anton Dolenc said in April 1990 that it was not
  poisoning but a psychogenic reaction. Albanian doctors and later Albanian accounts reject this;
  Kosovo's Institute for War Crimes (IKKL) published a catalogue on the poisonings in 2025.
- Claims of nerve agents (sarin) rest on a single later statement; no published toxicology confirms
  a specific substance.
*Proposed wording* (the strongest the sources support as fact): **"In March 1990 thousands of
Albanian pupils collapsed in their schools. Albanian doctors held that they had been poisoned;
the Serbian authorities denied it and called it hysteria."** The source tab gives both sides.
Stating "they were poisoned" as an established fact needs a published finding to cite; I will look
for one (IKKL catalogue, medical literature of 1990–91) and change the wording if it exists.

**D37 · Naming the UÇK.**
The UÇK appears at the end of Era VI and is central to Era VII. You chose "freedom fighters" for
the Kaçaks (D7).
*Recommendation:* use its own name, "Ushtria Çlirimtare e Kosovës (UÇK)" / "Kosovo Liberation Army
(KLA)", and describe it as "armed resistance", with no other label either way. The full treatment
of the UÇK, including the sensitive points, comes in the Era VII note.

**D40 · The camcorder date stamp.**
Era VI shows a VHS date stamp (e.g. "MAR 23 1989"). A stamp tells the viewer the footage was shot
that day.
*Recommendation:* show a stamp **only when the image really is from that date**. Scenes that use a
modern photograph, a document or a map show no stamp. Otherwise the effect would quietly fake
archive footage.
